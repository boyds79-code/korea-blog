Walking into a Korean jjimjilbang for the first time can feel intimidating. These 24-hour spa complexes are a cornerstone of Korean social life, but they operate on unspoken rules that locals take for granted. This guide walks you through exactly what to expect, what to do (and not do), and how to navigate your first visit with confidence—so you can relax and enjoy one of Korea's most rewarding cultural experiences.

## What Exactly Is a Jjimjilbang?

A jjimjilbang is more than just a sauna. Think of it as a spa, bathhouse, sleeping lounge, and social club rolled into one sprawling facility.

Most jjimjilbangs have two main sections: **gender-segregated bathing areas** (where you're fully nude) and **communal spaces** (where everyone wears provided uniforms). The bathing areas include hot tubs, cold plunge pools, body scrub stations, and showers. The communal areas feature saunas of different temperatures and materials (clay, salt, ice), nap rooms, snack bars, and sometimes even karaoke or gaming rooms.

Entry typically costs between **₩10,000–₩15,000** (roughly $7–$11 USD) for basic access, with additional fees for services like body scrubs or massages.

## What to Bring (and What You Don't Need)

The jjimjilbang provides almost everything, which is part of the appeal.

**You'll receive at check-in:**
- Two towels (one small, one large)
- Loose-fitting shorts and t-shirt uniform
- A locker key (usually worn as a wristband)

![Korean spa essentials including towels and amenities](/images/blog/korean-jjimjilbang-etiquette-your-first-timers-guide-to-sauna-culture/2.jpg)

**Bring from home:**
- Your own toiletries if you're picky (though basic soap and shampoo are provided)
- A hair tie if you have long hair
- Makeup remover and skincare
- A small plastic bag for wet items

**Leave behind:**
- Modesty. Seriously—the bathing areas require full nudity, and swimsuits are not allowed. This is non-negotiable and hygienic.

> 💡 Tip: Many jjimjilbangs rent "luxury" towels for ₩1,000–₩2,000 that are significantly bigger and softer than the standard-issue ones.

## The Step-by-Step First Visit

### Entering and Checking In

Pay at the front desk and receive your uniform, towels, and locker key. The key has a number—that's your locker in the gender-segregated changing room. Some facilities require you to remove your shoes and place them in cubbies before checking in.

Head to your gender's changing room (남 for men, 여 for women). Find your numbered locker, strip completely, and store everything except your small towel and toiletries.

## Bathing Area Protocol: Where Most Mistakes Happen

This is the most important section. The bathing area has strict but logical etiquette rules centered on cleanliness.

### Shower First—Thoroughly

Before you even think about entering a tub, **you must shower completely**. Find a shower station (low stools and detachable showerheads), sit down, and scrub yourself from head to toe with soap.

This isn't optional. You're sharing water with others, and Koreans consider it deeply disrespectful—even disgusting—to enter a communal bath without washing first.

### Using the Baths and Saunas

Once clean, you're free to rotate between hot baths (often 38–42°C), cold plunge pools (around 10–15°C), and dry saunas. Move between temperatures to stimulate circulation—this is the core ritual.

**Key rules:**
- Tie long hair back; don't let it touch the water
- Your small towel should not go into the pools (leave it at the edge or fold it on your head)
- Don't splash, swim, or be loud
- Rinse off briefly if you've been sweating heavily in a sauna before re-entering a bath

### The Body Scrub (Seshin)

This is optional but iconic. An ajumma (typically a middle-aged woman in the women's side) will scrub you down on a waterproof table using a rough mitt called an Italy towel, removing shocking amounts of dead skin.

It costs extra (usually **₩15,000–₩30,000**) and is NOT gentle. You'll be positioned, flipped, and scrubbed like a piece of furniture—but you'll leave feeling absurdly smooth. Book it at the front desk or with attendants in the bathing area.

## The Communal Areas: Where You Can Relax

After bathing, dry off, put on your uniform, and head to the communal floors. This is where jjimjilbangs become social.

You'll find multiple specialty sauna rooms—some blazing hot (70–90°C), others icy cold, some infused with jade, charcoal, or salt. Spend 10–15 minutes in each, then rest in the cooling lounge areas. Many people nap here on mats or recliners; it's completely normal to spend hours or even overnight.

**Communal space etiquette:**
- Keep noise low, especially in sleeping areas
- Don't hog space or equipment
- Dispose of your own snack trash
- If you see someone with a sheep-towel turban on their head, don't stare—it's just a traditional (and adorable) way to wrap the small towel

## Common First-Timer Mistakes to Avoid

Even with preparation, beginners often slip up. Here's what to watch for:

| Mistake | Why It's a Problem | What to Do Instead |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Wearing underwear in the bath | Cultural taboo; seen as unhygienic | Full nudity only in bathing areas |
| Not showering before bathing | Contaminates shared water | Thorough soap-and-rinse first |
| Taking photos | Illegal in nude areas; privacy violation | Leave phone in locker |
| Skipping the cold plunge | Misses half the health benefit | Alternate hot and cold for circulation |
| Leaving early | You paid for 12+ hours of access | Stay, nap, eat—make a night of it |

## What About Mixed Groups or Families?

The bathing areas are always gender-segregated, period. If you're visiting with friends of another gender or a mixed family, you'll split up for the bathing portion and reunite in the communal uniform areas.

Very young children (typically under 5–7 years) of either gender may accompany a parent into the opposite-gender bathing area, but this becomes less acceptable as kids get older.

## Making the Most of Your Visit

A jjimjilbang isn't a quick in-and-out experience. Locals often spend **4–8 hours** or stay overnight. Embrace the slow pace.

Try the snack bar—**sikhye** (sweet rice drink) and **maekshik** (boiled eggs) are the classic post-sauna combo. Rotate between hot and cold a few times. Nap if you're tired. Read, chat quietly, or just zone out.

The magic of a jjimjilbang isn't any single activity—it's the unhurried stretching of time, the shedding of daily stress along with dead skin, and the peculiar intimacy of being unadorned among strangers who are doing exactly the same thing.

## Before You Go

Check online reviews or ask your accommodation for recommendations. Some jjimjilbangs are sleek and modern (like Dragon Hill Spa or Siloam in Seoul), while others are neighborhood spots with more character and fewer tourists.

Arrive with an open mind and a willingness to be briefly uncomfortable. The first moment of public nudity is awkward for most foreigners—but within ten minutes, you'll realize nobody is looking at you, everyone is just as naked, and the whole thing is far less weird than you imagined.

That's when the real relaxation begins.
