If you've seen recent headlines about North Korea's leader pledging support for Russia or backing Moscow's military efforts, you might be wondering: why are these two countries allies at all? The relationship can seem puzzling from the outside, especially when most news about North Korea focuses on its isolation. Here's the historical, political, and cultural context that explains why Pyongyang and Moscow remain close—and what that means for the Korean Peninsula.

## The Historical Roots: How Russia Created North Korea

The story starts in **1945**, when the Soviet Union and the United States divided the Korean Peninsula along the 38th parallel after Japan's defeat in World War II. The Soviets occupied the North and immediately set about creating a communist state in their image.

Moscow hand-picked **Kim Il-sung**—grandfather of current leader Kim Jong-un—to lead North Korea. Soviet advisors helped build the country's government structure, military, and economy entirely from scratch. The North Korean state wasn't just influenced by the Soviet Union; it was essentially a Soviet creation.

This patron-client relationship deepened during the **Korean War (1950-1953)**. While China sent ground troops, the Soviet Union provided critical air support, weapons, military training, and economic aid. Thousands of Soviet pilots flew combat missions over Korea, though Moscow kept this secret for decades.

![Soviet-era monument in Pyongyang](/images/korea/why-north-korea-and-russia-are-allies-a-guide-for-outsiders/2.jpg)

Even after the war, the USSR remained North Korea's largest trade partner and aid provider throughout the Cold War. Soviet oil, food, and industrial equipment kept the North Korean economy functioning. Thousands of North Korean students studied in Soviet universities, and the countries signed a mutual defense treaty in 1961.

## What Happened After the Soviet Collapse

When the Soviet Union dissolved in **1991**, North Korea lost its economic lifeline almost overnight. The flow of cheap oil and food stopped. The result was catastrophic: North Korea's economy contracted by an estimated 50% during the 1990s, leading to a famine that killed hundreds of thousands (possibly over a million) people.

Russia under Boris Yeltsin largely ignored North Korea through the 1990s, focused on its own economic crisis and pursuing closer ties with the West. The mutual defense treaty wasn't formally cancelled, but Moscow made clear it was no longer in effect.

This abandonment is why North Korea accelerated its nuclear weapons program—it no longer had a superpower guaranteeing its security. It also helps explain the regime's deep suspicion of promises from larger powers.

## The Relationship Today: Why They Need Each Other

Under Vladimir Putin, Russia gradually re-engaged with North Korea starting in the early 2000s. But the relationship remained relatively low-key until recently, when both countries found themselves increasingly isolated from the West.

### What North Korea Gets From Russia

**Economic lifelines**: Russia provides fuel, food, and opportunities for North Korean workers to earn hard currency (though this has been restricted by UN sanctions). Even modest trade matters enormously to North Korea's struggling economy.

**Diplomatic cover**: As a permanent UN Security Council member, Russia can block or water down sanctions against North Korea. Moscow has vetoed or opposed several recent attempts to tighten restrictions.

**Technology and expertise**: Historically, Russia provided missile and satellite technology. Recent reports suggest North Korea may be receiving help with its space program and advanced weapons systems.

### What Russia Gets From North Korea

**Military supplies**: This has become especially significant recently. North Korea reportedly has massive stockpiles of Soviet-era artillery shells and rockets—exactly what Russia needs for its military operations. Unlike most countries, North Korea faces no domestic political pressure to avoid helping Russia.

**A diplomatic ally**: North Korea is one of the few countries to explicitly back Russia's actions in Ukraine. For Moscow, this vocal support matters symbolically, even if North Korea carries little global influence.

**Leverage with the West**: The closer Russia gets to North Korea, the more it complicates things for the US, which wants to denuclearize the Korean Peninsula.

## What This Means for South Korea and the Region

For South Korea, the strengthening Russia-North Korea relationship is deeply concerning for several practical reasons.

First, it undermines the international sanctions regime that Seoul has supported for years. If Russia helps North Korea evade sanctions, it makes the entire strategy less effective and prolongs the North's nuclear threat.

Second, any military technology flowing from Russia to North Korea—whether missile guidance systems, satellite technology, or submarine capabilities—directly threatens South Korea's security. The two Koreas remain technically at war (the 1953 armistice was a ceasefire, not a peace treaty), and Pyongyang regularly threatens Seoul.

Third, it complicates South Korea's own diplomatic position. Seoul has traditionally maintained decent relations with Moscow, but Russia's closer ties with Pyongyang force difficult choices. When Russia pressures South Korea not to provide military aid to Ukraine, the implied threat is often: "or we'll help North Korea more."

> 💡 **Context**: The Korean War never officially ended—just paused. North and South Korea are still technically in a state of war, which is why the North-Russia relationship matters so much for Seoul's security.

## Understanding the Pattern: Small States and Great Powers

The North Korea-Russia relationship follows a familiar pattern throughout Korean history: a smaller Korean state aligning with a larger continental power (traditionally China or Russia) to balance against other threats, especially from across the sea (historically Japan, now the US-South Korea alliance).

North Korea's geographic position makes it strategically significant. It shares borders with both China and Russia, and controls territory close to vital shipping routes and military installations. This gives Pyongyang leverage despite its economic weakness—great powers have reasons to keep it afloat.

For centuries, Korea was known as the "shrimp among whales," caught between larger powers. North Korea's leadership is acutely aware of this history and plays stronger neighbors off each other when possible. The relationship with Russia is partly genuine ideological alignment, partly cold strategic calculation.

## The Bottom Line for Outsiders

When you see headlines about North Korea backing Russia or the two countries expanding ties, understand it's not a new friendship—it's the continuation of a relationship that goes back **nearly 80 years**. Russia literally created the North Korean state, and despite the post-Soviet rupture, both countries now find mutual benefit in their alignment.

For South Koreans, these aren't distant geopolitical abstractions. North Korea sits just 50 kilometers from Seoul, and anything that strengthens Pyongyang's military capabilities or helps it evade sanctions is an immediate security concern. The Russia-North Korea partnership directly affects whether families can reunite, whether war remains unthinkable, and whether the peninsula moves toward peace or further entrenchment.

Understanding this context helps make sense of news that might otherwise seem random or confusing—and shows why events involving North Korea matter far beyond the peninsula itself.
