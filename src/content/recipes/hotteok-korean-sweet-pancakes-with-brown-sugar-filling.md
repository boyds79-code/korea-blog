These crispy-edged, chewy pancakes are one of Korea's most beloved street snacks, especially popular in cold weather when the hot, melted sugar filling is pure comfort. The magic is in the contrast: a soft, yeasted dough that gets golden and crisp on the outside while the brown sugar-cinnamon filling melts into sweet syrup inside. The trickiest part—and what makes or breaks hotteok—is sealing the filling so it doesn't leak out during cooking.

![Hotteok ingredients laid out](/images/recipes/hotteok-korean-sweet-pancakes-with-brown-sugar-filling/ingredients.jpg)

## Make the Dough

In a large mixing bowl, combine the warm water, instant yeast, and white sugar. Stir and **let it sit for about 5 minutes until foamy**—this tells you your yeast is alive and active. Add the flour, salt, and 2 tablespoons of oil. Mix with a wooden spoon or your hands until a sticky, shaggy dough forms. Unlike bread dough, hotteok dough stays quite sticky and soft—don't add extra flour trying to make it smooth.

**Knead in the bowl for 2-3 minutes** until the dough comes together. Oil your hands lightly if it's too sticky to handle. Cover the bowl with a damp kitchen towel or plastic wrap and **let it rise in a warm spot for 1 hour, or until roughly doubled in size**. The dough should be puffy and soft.

## Prepare the Filling

While the dough rises, mix the brown sugar, chopped nuts, cinnamon, and sesame seeds (if using) in a small bowl. Keep this mixture dry and ready—moisture will make it harder to seal later. Some cooks like to add a tiny pinch of flour to the filling mixture to help absorb the melting sugar, which can help prevent leaks, but it's optional.

## Shape and Fill

Once the dough has risen, oil your hands generously—this dough is sticky by design. Divide it into 6-8 equal portions (about golf-ball size). Take one portion and flatten it gently in your oiled palm into a disc about 3-4 inches wide, keeping the edges slightly thicker than the center.

**Place about 1-2 tablespoons of filling in the center of the disc.** Here's the critical sealing technique: gather the edges of the dough up and over the filling, **pinching them together firmly at the top like a little pouch**. Then twist the gathered top and press it flat against the pancake. The key is to make sure there are **no gaps or thin spots** where the sugar syrup can escape. Press the seam side down and gently flatten the ball into a disc about 1/2 inch thick. The filling should be completely enclosed.

> 💡 **Tip:** The most common mistake is overfilling or not pinching the seam tightly enough. If sugar leaks out, it'll burn on the pan and make a mess. When in doubt, use slightly less filling and seal very firmly.

## Cook the Hotteok

Heat a nonstick or well-seasoned skillet over **medium to medium-low heat**. Add about 1 tablespoon of oil. Place one or two filled dough discs seam-side down in the pan. **Cook for 2-3 minutes until the bottom is golden brown**, then flip carefully.

After flipping, use a flat spatula or a hotteok press (a flat metal spatula works great) to **gently press the pancake flat—about 1/4 inch thick**. This spreads the melted filling and creates those crispy edges. **Cook for another 2-3 minutes until both sides are deep golden brown and crispy**. The sugar inside will be bubbling hot, so let them cool for a minute before biting in. Repeat with the remaining dough, adding a little oil to the pan between batches.

## Serving and Variations

Hotteok is best eaten warm, when the filling is still gooey and the edges are crisp. Let them cool for just **1-2 minutes to avoid burning your mouth** on the molten sugar. They're traditionally a hand-held snack, often served in a paper cup on the street.

If you can't find instant yeast, active dry yeast works too—just proof it a bit longer. No brown sugar? White sugar mixed with a little molasses or even just white sugar with extra cinnamon will work. For a savory twist, some cooks fill hotteok with japchae (glass noodles) or vegetables, but the sweet version is the classic. Leftover hotteok can be reheated in a dry skillet to crisp them back up, though they're never quite as good as fresh.
