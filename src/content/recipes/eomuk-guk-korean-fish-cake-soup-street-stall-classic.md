There's something deeply comforting about eomuk guk—the kind of soup you grab from a street cart on a cold afternoon, steam rising from a paper cup while you fish out skewered fish cakes with chopsticks. The broth is light but savory, the fish cakes tender and slightly bouncy, and the whole thing comes together in about twenty minutes. This is 길거리 분식 (street snack) cooking at its finest: minimal ingredients, maximum flavor, no fuss.

![Ingredients for Korean fish cake soup](/images/recipes/eomuk-guk-korean-fish-cake-soup-street-stall-classic/ingredients.jpg)

## Make the Anchovy-Kelp Broth (Yuksu)

The backbone of this soup is a simple anchovy-kelp stock, which is how most Korean home cooks and street vendors build savory, umami-rich broth without spending hours on it. Start by **removing the heads and black guts from the dried anchovies**—this step takes two minutes and prevents any bitterness. Pinch off the heads, split each anchovy lengthwise, and flick out the dark intestinal thread.

Add the cleaned anchovies and the piece of dried kelp to a pot with the water. Bring to a **boil over medium-high heat, then reduce to a gentle simmer and cook for 8-10 minutes**. You'll see the water turn golden and smell that oceanic, umami aroma. **Remove and discard the kelp after about 5 minutes** (it gets slimy if left too long), but let the anchovies keep simmering the full time.

Strain out the anchovies and discard them. You now have a clean, flavorful broth—this is your base. If you can't find dried anchovies, you can use a couple of teaspoons of dashida (Korean anchovy soup stock powder) dissolved in water, but the real thing gives a cleaner, more authentic taste.

> 💡 **Tip:** Don't skip removing the anchovy guts. It's the single most common reason homemade anchovy broth tastes bitter instead of sweet and savory.

## Add the Fish Cakes and Simmer

Return the strained broth to the pot and bring it back to a simmer. Add the **sliced garlic and fish cakes**, arranging them so they're mostly submerged. If you want that street-cart vibe, thread a few fish cake pieces onto wooden skewers before adding them—it's not just for looks; it makes them easier to pull out and eat.

**Simmer for 5-6 minutes** until the fish cakes are heated through and have absorbed some of the broth. They should look slightly plumped and glossy. Taste the broth now and season with the soy sauce and salt. Start with less salt than you think—you can always add more, and some fish cakes are already a bit salty.

## Finish and Serve

Stir in the **green onion pieces** and let them wilt for **1 minute**. Turn off the heat. The broth should taste clean, gently salty, and just a little sweet from the kelp and anchovies. If you like a hint of heat, drop in a dried red chili or a pinch of chili flakes.

## Serving and Variations

Ladle the soup into bowls, making sure each person gets plenty of fish cakes and a few green onion pieces. This is excellent on its own as a light meal or snack, but it's also a classic side to kimbap, tteokbokki, or any 분식 spread. Some street vendors add a splash of rice wine (cheongju) or a drizzle of sesame oil at the end—both are optional but add a nice layer of flavor.

**Can't find Korean fish cakes?** Look for them frozen in Asian grocery stores (sometimes labeled "odeng" or "eomuk"). In a pinch, Japanese kamaboko or narutomaki work, though the texture is slightly firmer. If you're really stuck, imitation crab sticks can stand in, but slice them thicker and add them at the very end so they don't fall apart.

Leftovers keep well in the fridge for a day or two—just reheat gently. The fish cakes will soak up more broth as they sit, so you may want to add a splash of water when reheating.
